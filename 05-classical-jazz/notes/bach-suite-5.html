Bach — Suite No. 5 in C minor, BWV 1011

The one where you retune the instrument

Note by Odile Rannoch · 30 August 2026

6 min read · 794 words

Scordatura drops the A string a whole tone, and the cello stops sounding like itself for twenty-five minutes. That is not a side effect. It is the piece.

The fifth of Bach's six suites asks the player to tune the top string down from A to G before starting. It sounds like a small instruction. In practice it changes almost everything. The brightest string on the instrument becomes duller and slacker; chords that would be awkward in normal tuning fall under the hand; and the whole cello takes on a darker, more veiled sound, as if the piece were being played in the next room. When I tune down backstage the instrument feels, for a few minutes, like someone else's.

What that tuning gives the music is resonance. C minor is not a key the cello naturally rings in, and in standard tuning much of this suite would sound effortful. With the top string lowered, the open strings sit inside the harmony, and they sympathise with the notes around them. A single low note at the start of the Prélude seems to hang in the air longer than it should. That is the tuning, and the room, doing some of the work.

The Prélude is built like a French overture: a slow, dotted, ceremonial opening, and then a long fugue. A fugue for one instrument that can mostly play one line at a time is a kind of conjuring trick, and Bach is completely unembarrassed about it. The voices enter one after another and then have to take turns, so the listener is asked to remember a line that has stopped sounding while another one continues. I think of it as the most generous passage in the suites, because it assumes you are paying attention and trusts you to finish the counterpoint in your head.

After that the dances arrive in their usual order — Allemande, Courante, Sarabande, a pair of Gavottes and a Gigue — but they do not behave as dances usually do. The Courante is jagged. The Gavottes are the only part of the suite that sounds as though anyone might actually dance to it, and even they seem to be dancing slightly out of step.

For years I played the Sarabande slowly and beautifully and got very tired of it. I now play it a little faster and as plainly as I can bear, and I think it is sadder that way.

Then there is the Sarabande. It has no chords at all: a single line, mostly in slow, even notes, that climbs and falls and never resolves quite where you expect it to. On paper it looks like the simplest thing Bach wrote for the instrument. On stage it is the hardest two or three minutes of the evening, because there is nowhere to hide and nothing to decorate. For years I played it slowly and beautifully and got very tired of it. I now play it a little faster and as plainly as I can bear, and I think it is sadder that way.

The Gigue at the end is short, and in the minor key it sounds less like a celebration than a sign-off. I have heard players rush it to get the audience clapping. I would rather they didn't clap immediately.

There is a practical cost to all this that audiences rarely see. A string tuned down a whole tone loses tension, and a slack string is less stable; it drifts, particularly under hot stage lights, and it needs time to settle. When I play the fifth suite in the middle of a recital I retune in the interval and then play a few minutes of scales backstage to let the string find its new pitch. When I programme it straight after another piece, I tell the audience I am retuning, because a minute of silence with a performer fiddling at the pegs looks like a problem unless you know it is the beginning of the piece.

Bach also made a version of this suite for lute, which survives in his own hand, and players have long used that manuscript to argue about how the cello version ought to go — which notes are chords, which are ornaments, where the bass line really sits. I have read those arguments with interest and changed my mind several times. The practical answer, for me, is that the cello version already contains its own shadow: the lowered string means half of what you hear is resonance rather than notes, and no edition can mark that for you.

If you have not heard this suite in a room before, sit closer than you would for a concerto. Much of it is quiet, and a surprising amount of the sound is the instrument answering itself. And if the cello sounds strange at the start, that is deliberate. Give it a minute. By the Sarabande it will sound like the only way the instrument was ever supposed to sound.
