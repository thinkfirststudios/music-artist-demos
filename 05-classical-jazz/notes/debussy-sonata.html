Debussy — Sonata for Cello and Piano, L. 135

Eleven minutes, and not one of them settled

Note by Odile Rannoch · 12 July 2026

5 min read · 814 words

Debussy called it Pierrot angry with the moon, then withdrew the description. I have played it eleven ways and I still do not know which one is right.

The sonata was written in the summer of 1915, by the sea, during the war, by a composer who was ill and knew it. It was meant to be the first of six sonatas for different combinations of instruments; he finished three. On the title page he signed himself not simply as Claude Debussy but as a French musician, which in 1915 was not a neutral thing to write. The piece that follows is eleven minutes long and seems, most of the time, to be refusing to explain itself.

It is in three movements, and the last two run together without a break. The Prologue opens with the piano alone, a few grand, deliberately old-fashioned chords, as if the piece intended to be a French Baroque sonata and then thought better of it. The cello enters speaking rather than singing. Almost nothing in this movement settles for more than a bar or two. Phrases start and are interrupted. A melody appears and is abandoned. The dynamics are marked with extraordinary precision and change constantly, which makes it very easy to play too carefully and very hard to play naturally.

The Sérénade is where the Pierrot story, whether or not Debussy meant it, earns its keep. The cello is asked to pluck, to strum, to imitate a guitar or a mandolin, to play sul ponticello so the sound goes glassy and thin, and then, very occasionally, to sing properly for a moment before undercutting itself. It is a serenade by someone who doesn't believe the person on the balcony is listening. I have played it as comedy and as something close to despair, and both versions have worked in some rooms and failed in others.

The Finale bursts out of it: fast, bright, rhythmically restless, with a slower, stranger passage in the middle that seems to wander off into a different piece entirely before remembering where it is. And then it ends abruptly, with two quick gestures, as though Debussy had simply decided that was enough.

It is a serenade by someone who doesn't believe the person on the balcony is listening.

The difficulty for performers is that the score is both very exact and very open. Every hairpin is marked. The tempos are not. You can make this sonata sound Romantic, and it will sound like a lesser Romantic piece. You can play it drily and objectively, and it will sound like a clever exercise. The version I believe in, at least this season, is somewhere cooler than fashion prefers: very exact about the notation, and almost uninterested in charm.

The piano part deserves more attention than it usually gets in notes like this one. It is not an accompaniment. For long stretches the piano sets the harmony, the colour and the tempo, and the cello comments on it from the side. Debussy was a pianist, and he wrote the part as though he intended to play it himself, full of held pedal effects and chords that only make sense if they are allowed to ring. A pianist who plays it as a supporting role will make the cello sound as though it is shouting in an empty room. Hélène plays it as the other half of an argument, which is the only way I have found that works.

It is also worth saying what the sonata is not. It is not an Impressionist piece in the soft-focus sense that word has come to suggest. There is very little haze in it. The textures are dry and clear, the rhythms are sharp, and the harmony, however unusual, is placed exactly. The composer Debussy admired from the eighteenth century, Rameau, is closer to this music than Monet is, and I find it helps to think of the whole piece as a very old kind of French music played by someone who has lost patience with it.

When it is programmed, the sonata tends to be put first in a recital, as a short and brilliant opener. I understand the reasoning, but I now prefer it in the middle of an evening, after something warm and long-breathed, where its refusals register as refusals rather than as mere cleverness. In French Rooms it follows the Fauré Élégie, and the contrast between the two is a large part of the point.

Hélène and I recorded it for Late Trains over two days, and the take we used is the one where we were most annoyed with each other, which I think is audible and appropriate. We have played it perhaps forty times since and it has not settled. I don't expect it to.

If you are hearing it for the first time, listen for the silences. Debussy writes rests the way other composers write notes, and a good performance lets them last just slightly too long.
