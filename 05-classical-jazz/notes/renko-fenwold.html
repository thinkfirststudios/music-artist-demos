Ilya Renko — Fenwold (2024), for solo cello and fixed media

Writing for a cellist and a tape machine

Note by Odile Rannoch · 3 May 2026

7 min read · 772 words

Ilya sent me forty seconds of a recording of an empty room and asked what I could do against it. This is a note about what I couldn't.

Fenwold is a piece for solo cello and fixed media, which is the polite term for a recording that plays back through speakers while I play live. Ilya Renko wrote it for me in 2024, and it takes its name from a farmhouse where he spent a winter recording the sounds of rooms nobody was using: a kitchen after dark, an unheated upstairs bedroom, a stairwell. The fixed-media part is made almost entirely from those recordings, slowed, layered and very lightly tuned. There is no melody in it. There is air, a creak, a distant engine, and, once, a clock that he kept because he couldn't get rid of it.

The forty seconds he sent me first were the stairwell. His question was simple: what does the cello do in a room like that? My first answers were wrong. I played long, beautiful notes over it and the recording swallowed them; I played busy, rhythmic material and the recording made me sound like someone talking loudly in a library. For about a month I sent him voice memos of failures.

What we eventually found was that the cello had to behave like another sound in the room rather than a performer in front of it. Much of the solo part is played very quietly, often with the bow close to the fingerboard so the tone is soft and breathy, and a great deal of it is timed not to a beat but to events in the tape — a creak, the engine passing. There is a score, and it is precise, but it tells me what to listen for as often as what to play.

The middle of the piece is a passage Ilya calls the kitchen, where the tape almost disappears and I play a slow, low line that gradually picks up the resonance of the recording as it fades back in. By the end of that section it is genuinely hard, in the hall, to tell which sounds are coming from the cello and which from the speakers. People ask afterwards whether the tape was doing something clever. It wasn't. It was the room.

A recording does not care. It will arrive at the clock at 9:12 whether I am ready or not.

What I couldn't do, and what the piece taught me not to try, was make the tape responsive. My instinct as a performer is to lead: to push the tempo when something feels alive and to hold back when it doesn't. A recording does not care. It will arrive at the clock at 9:12 whether I am ready or not. For the first dozen performances that felt like a loss of freedom. Now it feels like the point. The piece is about being in a place that was there before you and will be there after you've gone, and a cellist who can't bend it to her will is exactly the right protagonist for that.

Practically, Fenwold needs a stereo pair of decent speakers placed behind and slightly either side of me, a simple playback cue from the side of the stage, and a hall that can get genuinely quiet. It doesn't need to be large; it is better in small rooms. I usually programme it between two Bach suites, where the silences on either side make it sound less like a novelty and more like a continuation.

There is a question I am asked after almost every performance, usually kindly: is it music? I understand why. There is no tune to take home, and for stretches the most active thing in the hall is the air conditioning. My answer has changed over the two years I've played it. At first I defended it on technical grounds, pointing at the score and the precision of the timings. Now I say that it is a piece about listening, and that the test of whether it has worked is not what you remember of the cello but whether, walking out into the street afterwards, the street sounds different for a minute or two. For most people, I think, it does.

Ilya and I have talked about a second piece, for cello and a recording of an occupied room, with voices and cutlery and a radio on. He thinks it would be a comedy. I am not sure he's wrong, and I am not sure I would be able to play it with a straight face.

The recording on the tape includes one sound I still can't identify. Ilya claims not to know either. I have decided not to ask again.
